Picture this

It is 8 pm on a Friday in downtown San Francisco and you open a map app: "restaurants within 500 m". Your phone sends two numbers, 37.776720 and -122.416730, and within a blink you get a ranked list of nearby places. Out of 200 million businesses on the planet, the system had to find the few dozen inside a small circle around you, while 100 million daily users fire around 5,000 searches per second, spiking in exactly the dense neighbourhoods where businesses cluster. The catch is that databases are brilliant at sorting along one line but clumsy at asking "what is near this point in two dimensions?"

1 Macro Structure & Dependency Map

The system is two services on one database cluster: a read-only Location-Based Service that answers "what is near me" and a Business Service that manages the businesses themselves. The intellectual heart is the geospatial index that turns 2D proximity into a 1D lookup.

  • Geospatial Index — the whole problem is making 2D "nearby" searchable; every other choice follows from which index you pick
    • Hash family: even grid, geohash
      • Geohash: 12 precision levels; shared prefix = nearby
      • Boundary issues → also search 8 neighbours
    • Tree family: quadtree, Google S2, R-tree
      • Quadtree splits until ≤100 businesses per leaf
      • S2: Hilbert curve, geofencing
    • Naive 2D search: lat/long range query (baseline to reject)
  • Location-Based Service (LBS) — the hot, read-only, stateless path that handles ~5,000 QPS
    • GET /v1/search/nearby with latitude, longitude, radius (default 5000 m)
    • Radius → geohash length → own cell + 8 neighbours
    • Parallel Redis calls, then rank by distance
  • Business Service & Data Store — the source of truth for business details and owner edits
    • Business CRUD APIs (GET/POST/PUT/DELETE /v1/businesses)
    • MySQL primary-replica cluster; business table sharded by business ID
      • Geohash index table fits one server: use read replicas, not shards
    • Updates not real-time; batch processed daily
      • Replica lag is acceptable
  • Caching & Deployment — what makes the read path fast and resilient under peaks
    • Cache key = geohash (not raw coordinates)
    • Two Redis caches: geohash → business IDs; business_id → details
    • Multi-region deployment
      • GDPR / CCPA privacy compliance

The geospatial index is the hub because it is what LBS queries, what the cache is keyed on, and what determines whether the data must be rebuilt (quadtree) or simply re-hashed (geohash). LBS and Business Service are split because their traffic shapes differ: LBS is massive, read-only and stateless, while Business Service is lower-volume and owns writes.

2 Black-Box Cause & Effect Flow

A nearby search is a funnel: coordinates become a cell code, the cell code becomes a list of IDs, the IDs become rich details, and the details become a ranked list.

Load Balancer

Input: HTTP requests from clients.

Output: /search/nearby routed to LBS; /businesses/{id} routed to Business Service.

A fork in the road: "looking for places?" goes left, "looking at one place?" goes right.

→ feeds into: LBS or Business Service

LBS: radius to geohash

Input: latitude, longitude, radius.

Output: The user's geohash at the right precision plus its 8 neighbouring geohashes.

Turns "this exact spot and 500 m around it" into "this tile and the eight tiles touching it".

→ feeds into: Geohash Redis

Geohash Redis

Input: A list of 9 geohashes.

Output: Business IDs in each of those cells, fetched with parallel queries.

A set of pigeonholes labelled by tile code; each holds the IDs of businesses located in that tile.

→ feeds into: Business Info Redis

Business Info Redis

Input: Business IDs.

Output: Full details: name, address, reviews, location.

The card catalogue: give it an ID, get the full card.

→ feeds into: LBS ranking

LBS ranking

Input: Candidate businesses with coordinates.

Output: Results sorted by distance from the user, returned to the client.

The tiles gave a rough neighbourhood; ranking measures the real distance and orders the list.

→ feeds into: the client's map

Business Service & DB cluster

Input: Owner create/update/delete requests; customer detail views.

Output: Writes to the MySQL primary, replicated to replicas and synced into the Redis caches, batch processed daily.

The back office that edits the records; the front counter (Redis) gets its copies refreshed on a schedule.

→ feeds into: Redis caches used by LBS

Follow one search

  1. You search for restaurants within 500 m. The client sends latitude 37.776720, longitude -122.416730, radius 500 m to the load balancer.
  2. The load balancer sees /search/nearby and forwards the request to one of the stateless LBS servers.
  3. LBS consults the radius-to-precision table: 500 m maps to geohash length 6. It encodes your coordinates into a 6-character geohash.
  4. Because you might be standing near a cell edge, LBS computes the 8 neighbouring geohashes, giving a list of 9: [my_geohash, neighbor1, ..., neighbor8].
  5. LBS queries the Geohash Redis server for all 9 in parallel. Each returns a list of business IDs, like 9q9hvu → [343, 347, 112].
  6. LBS fetches full details for those IDs from the Business Info Redis server.
  7. LBS computes the distance from you to each business, sorts them, and returns the ranked list.
  8. Later, a restaurant owner updates their hours through Business Service. The write goes to the MySQL primary and is picked up in the daily batch, so up to a day of staleness is expected and harmless.

3 Purpose & Concept Beacons

A proximity service converts two-dimensional location into a one-dimensional key (a geohash or a tree path) so that "what is near me?" becomes a fast, cacheable lookup instead of a scan.

Mental picture

Imagine the Earth covered in nested tiles, like a floor where each tile can be cut into four smaller tiles, and those into four more. Every tile has a postcode spelled out letter by letter, and longer postcodes mean smaller tiles: that postcode is the geohash. To find what is near you, you read your own postcode at the right length for your radius, then peek at the eight tiles touching yours (neighbour search). A pigeonhole wall holds the business IDs for each postcode (Geohash Redis), and a card catalogue holds each business's details (Business Info Redis). A quadtree is the same floor, but tiles are cut only where it is crowded, so a dense downtown gets tiny tiles and a desert gets huge ones.

4 Opportunistic Drilling

Almost every question lands on the index: why naive search fails, how geohash works and breaks, when a quadtree wins, and how the final architecture stitches it to Redis. Drill these four and you can reconstruct the rest.

Why naive 2D search fails, and the two index families

The intuitive approach draws a box around the user and asks the database for every business whose latitude is within the radius and whose longitude is within the radius. Without indexes that scans the whole table. With separate indexes on latitude and longitude it is only slightly better: each index returns a huge band (a horizontal strip and a vertical strip spanning the globe), and the database must intersect those two enormous sets.

The root problem is that a database index speeds search in one dimension only. So the better approach maps 2D space into one dimension. Two families do this. Hash-based: the even grid and geohash. Tree-based: quadtree, Google S2 and R-tree.

Common trap: proposing an even grid. Fixed-size cells ignore density: a downtown cell holds thousands of businesses while a rural or ocean cell holds none.

Geohash: postcodes for the planet

Split the world into four quadrants along the prime meridian and the equator, labelling each with two bits. Split each quadrant into four again, and keep going. Each step adds bits, alternating between longitude and latitude, and the resulting bit string is encoded as a short alphanumeric string. There are 12 precision levels; each extra character means a smaller cell.

Choosing precision is a table lookup: take the minimal geohash length that covers the radius. 500 m needs length 6; 1 km and 2 km need length 5; 5 km and 20 km need length 4. The useful property is that a longer shared prefix means two points are closer, so a prefix match finds everything in a cell.

The catch is at the edges. Two places can be metres apart yet share no prefix at all (for example on opposite sides of the equator), and two places can share a long prefix but sit in different cells. The fix is simple and always applied: search your own cell plus its 8 neighbours.

Quadtree: tiles that shrink where it is crowded

A quadtree recursively splits 2D space into four quadrants, and each internal node has exactly four children. It keeps splitting a region until no leaf holds more than a threshold of businesses (100 in the notes). Dense cities therefore get deep, fine-grained branches, while empty areas stop early with large leaves.

It is an in-memory structure built on each LBS server at startup. It needs only GBs of memory, so it fits on one server, but building it is O(n log n) and takes a few minutes for about 200 million businesses. During that time the server cannot serve traffic, so new releases must roll out incrementally to a subset of servers. Adding or updating a business either triggers an incremental rebuild (with lots of cache invalidation) or an on-the-fly update that needs locking.

Scaling, caching and the final architecture

The business table is sharded by business ID so data spreads evenly. The geohash index table is different: it maps geohash to business ID, one row per business, and it fits on a single server, so sharding adds complexity with no benefit. Read replicas handle the read load instead. The primary-replica setup can briefly serve stale data, which is fine because business information is not updated in real time and changes are batch processed daily.

For caching, raw coordinates make a poor key: GPS readings are imprecise and users move, so almost every request would miss. The geohash is stable, so it becomes the key. Two caches result: geohash → list of business IDs, and business_id → business details.

Gains

Stateless LBS scales horizontally; parallel Redis reads keep latency low; multi-region deployment handles peaks and outages.

Costs

Data is eventually consistent: a business edit is not visible until the daily batch and cache sync.

Why does indexing latitude and longitude separately still perform poorly?

A database index speeds search in only one dimension. Each index returns a huge strip of candidates, and the database must intersect those two large sets. Geospatial indexing solves this by mapping 2D location to a single 1D key.

What happens if LBS searches only the user's own geohash cell?

Businesses just across a cell boundary are missed, even if they are a few metres away, because nearby points need not share a prefix. That is why LBS always adds the 8 neighbouring cells.

A user wants restaurants within 500 m. Which geohash length, and why that one?

Length 6, from the radius-to-precision table. You pick the minimal length whose cells cover the radius, so the cell plus its neighbours captures the search circle without pulling in far too many candidates.

Compare geohash and quadtree for a "find the 5 closest gas stations" query.

Quadtree is better: it supports k-nearest searches and adapts cell size to density. Geohash is built for fixed-radius search and cannot dynamically resize its grid, although it is simpler and easier to update.

Why is the geohash a better cache key than the raw coordinates?

GPS coordinates are imprecise and change as users move, so exact coordinates rarely repeat and would almost always miss the cache. Many nearby users share the same geohash, making it a stable, high-hit-rate key.

Why shard the business table but not the geohash index table?

The business table holds rich details for 200 million businesses and benefits from even distribution by business ID. The geohash index is small enough to fit on one server, so sharding only adds complexity; read replicas handle the read load.

What operational risk does the quadtree introduce, and how is it handled?

Building it at startup takes a few minutes (O(n log n) for about 200 million businesses), during which the server cannot serve traffic. Releases are rolled out incrementally to a subset of servers at a time so capacity remains.
